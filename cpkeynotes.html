On the Dependability of ML for Security-Related Tasks

 Michael Reiter, James B. Duke Distinguished Professor in the Departments of Computer Science and Electrical & Computer Engineering at Duke University

Abstract: In this talk, we will survey our results over the past decade in evaluating ML's ability to perform tasks dependably, particularly tasks where ML-generated outputs impact security of systems or data. First, by casting authorization decisions as the task of classifying requests into those that should be permitted or denied, we review our results showing that ML classifiers tend to be susceptible to being fooled by adversaries in two important authorization contexts: facial recognition and malware detection. Second, we consider the ability of ML models to keep secrets, and put their weaknesses in doing so to a constructive use: enabling a data owner to audit an ML model for use of her data in training. In particular, we will detail our progress on developing general techniques for black-box, data-use auditing of ML models that enable the data owner to bound her false-detection rate.

Bio: Michael Reiter is a James B. Duke Distinguished Professor in the Departments of Computer Science and Electrical & Computer Engineering at Duke University. His research spans computer security and fault‑tolerant distributed systems. He is a Fellow of the ACM and IEEE and has received the Outstanding Contributions Award from the ACM Special Interest Group on Security, Audit and Control; the ACM CODASPY Lasting Research Award; and multiple Test of Time Awards from ACM CCS and from Intel for specific research contributions. More information can be found at https://reitermk.github.io.


Robust Power Management in AI-Centric Systems

 Pradip Bose, Distinguished Research Scientist and Manager of Efficient & Resilient Systems at IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center

Abstract: Ever since the onset of the so-called “power wall” in the early 2000’s, power management of processor chips (and all the way up to datacenters) has been a challenge. With the emergence of AI as the pre-dominant application driver of the 21 st century, this challenge has magnified in a dramatic manner. In this talk, we will examine the problem of ensuring reliable and secure power management in the small, scaled up all the way up to large-scale systems. We will discuss fault and threat models, with associated test and verification solution strategies. We will show that designing for scalability leads to interesting trade-offs across the dimensions of power, performance, reliability and security.

Bio: Pradip Bose is a Distinguished Research Scientist and Manager of Efficient & Resilient Systems at IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center. He holds a PhD from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. His team’s research focuses on systems architecture and domain-specific accelerators, with a focus on AI-centric systems, energy efficiency, reliability and security. He holds the title of Master Inventor at IBM and is a Fellow of the IEEE.


Self-Healing Mechanisms to Safeguard Critical Firmware Infrastructure

 Bernard McShea, Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), Information Processing Techniques Office (IPTO), Program Manager

Abstract: As a critical but often overlooked layer of the computing stack, firmware requires scalable, real-time resilience to counter rapid adversarial cycles. This session explores the AI-enabled transformation of the firmware security ecosystem and the methodologies required to achieve self-healing autonomy. By defining self-healing systems as fault-tolerant architectures that explicitly account for malicious actors, we propose a framework for on-system, near real-time patch generation and verification. Achieving "hitless" autonomous defense ensures that the speed of remediation exceeds the speed of exploitation. Ultimately, this capability shifts the economic balance of cyber warfare, empowering the defender through automated, machine-speed resilience.

Bio: Bernard McShea received his bachelor’s degree in computer science and mathematics from Beloit College, Beloit, WI, USA and his master’s degree in computer science from Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN, USA. His research interests include self-healing systems, energy-aware machine learning, edge compute, and distributed causal knowledge extraction. He founded the DARPA programs; Reclaiming Bus-based Systems During Compromise (Red-C) and Mapping Machine Learning to Physics (ML2P). He founded the DARPA Cyber First Aid SBIR, which developed a prototype self-healing system.
